Agent [Small talk]: This is a solid area of research. Here's what it covers:

**Linguistics foundation (turn-taking theory):**
- **Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson (1974)** — the foundational paper on conversational turn-taking. Key finding: conversations run on predictable rules about when it's appropriate to speak, and violations (interrupting too soon, leaving too long a gap, or asking too much before yielding the "floor") make people uncomfortable or disengaged. Applied to chatbots — asking several questions back-to-back without ever "yielding the floor" (i.e., without a moment where you just respond to what they said) breaks that natural rhythm and feels unnatural.

**Applied to UX/chatbot research:**
- **Pacing and response length matching** — studies on chatbot design show that matching your response length/depth to the user's input length increases perceived naturalness. If someone gives a short answer, following with a long multi-part question feels mismatched and can cause disengagement.

- **"One thing at a time" principle** — UX research on conversational interfaces (common in voice UI and chatbot design literature) consistently finds that stacking multiple questions in a single turn reduces completion rates. Single, sequential questions outperform bundled ones, even if the bundled version is technically fewer "turns."

- **Latency and pacing** — research on human-bot interaction found that if a bot responds too fast after a heavy question, it can feel robotic/uncaring; a very short pause or acknowledgment ("that sounds tough" before pivoting) improves perceived empathy and continued engagement.

- **Grounding theory (Clark & Brennan, 1991)** — communication research showing that conversations proceed smoothly when both parties confirm mutual understanding before moving on. In your context, that supports briefly restating what someone told you before your next question — closing the loop before opening a new one.

The common thread across all of this: **one idea per turn, acknowledge before advancing, match their energy/length.** That's a direct answer to your "how do I keep the depth but reduce bounce" problem — it's less about cutting the number of questions and more about pacing them so each one doesn't feel like a new demand.
